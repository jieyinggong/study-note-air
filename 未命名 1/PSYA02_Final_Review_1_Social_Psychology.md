# 1. Foundations of Social Psychology

## Social Psychology

The lecture defines **social psychology** as "the study of the causes and consequences of being social."

Humans are highly social and are considered **ultrasocial organisms**. Ultrasocial organisms form large societies, divide labour, and cooperate for mutual benefit. Social psychology therefore examines how other people, groups, norms, roles, and situations influence thought and behaviour.

The module has two central questions:

1. How do people interpret the behaviour of others?
2. How does the presence of others change individual behaviour?

# 2. Interpreting the Behaviour of Others

## Attribution Theory

**Attribution** is an explanation for the cause of behaviour.

People may attribute behaviour to:

- **Internal or dispositional causes:** personality, traits, ability, attitude, or other characteristics of the person.
- **External or situational causes:** environmental circumstances, social context, chance, or temporary conditions.

Example: Jane is late for work.

- Internal attribution: "Jane is irresponsible."
- External attribution: "Jane is stuck in traffic."

The key distinction is whether the explanation focuses on what the person is like or on what happened around the person.

## Kelley's Covariation Model of Attributions

Harold Kelley proposed that people consider three types of information when making internal or external attributions.

### Consistency

**Consistency** asks: Is the person's behaviour similar in the same context across time?

- High consistency: Alex is late to this class every week.
- Low consistency: Alex was late only today.

### Distinctiveness

**Distinctiveness** asks: Does the person behave similarly across different situations or contexts?

- High distinctiveness: Alex is late only to this class.
- Low distinctiveness: Alex is late to every class and appointment.

High distinctiveness means that the behaviour is distinctive to one particular situation.

### Consensus

**Consensus** asks: Is the behaviour similar to or different from other people's behaviour in the same situation?

- High consensus: Most students were late today.
- Low consensus: Only Alex was late.

### Attribution Pattern

| Pattern | Most Likely Attribution |
|---|---|
| High consistency, low distinctiveness, low consensus | Internal or dispositional |
| High consistency, high distinctiveness, high consensus | External or situational |
| Low consistency | A temporary or unusual circumstance; avoid a strong stable conclusion |

## Attribution Biases

### Fundamental Attribution Error

The **fundamental attribution error** is the tendency to attribute the behaviour of others to internal or dispositional causes rather than external or environmental causes.

Example: A customer assumes that a rude server has a rude personality and ignores the possibility that the server is exhausted or has just experienced a difficult interaction.

Key recognition cue: The observer explains another person's behaviour by personality and underestimates the situation.

### Actor-Observer Bias

The **actor-observer bias** is the tendency to explain one's own behaviour using situational or external causes, while explaining other people's behaviour using dispositional or internal causes.

Example:

- "I was late because traffic was terrible."
- "She was late because she is irresponsible."

The actor-observer bias includes a contrast between explanations for the self and explanations for others. The fundamental attribution error focuses specifically on overusing dispositional explanations for other people.

### Self-Serving Bias

The **self-serving bias** is the tendency to attribute one's successes to internal causes and one's failures to external causes.

- Success: "I earned a high grade because I am capable and worked hard."
- Failure: "I earned a low grade because the test was unfair."

This pattern may protect self-esteem. Unlike actor-observer bias, it specifically depends on whether the outcome is favourable or unfavourable.

### False Consensus Effect

The **false consensus effect** is the tendency to think that everyone agrees with us when they likely do not.

Example: "Everyone dislikes early-morning classes" may simply project the speaker's own preference onto other people.

### Attribution Bias Comparison

| Example | Concept |
|---|---|
| "He is late because he is irresponsible." | Fundamental attribution error |
| "I am late because of traffic; he is late because he is lazy." | Actor-observer bias |
| "My success reflects my ability; my failure reflects an unfair test." | Self-serving bias |
| "Most people must share my opinion." | False consensus effect |

# 3. Impression Formation

## Impression Formation and First Impressions

**Impression formation** is how people create opinions about other people.

A **first impression** is the initial information learned about someone. First impressions are made quickly, often within seconds or minutes, and can be enduring.

## Primacy Effect

The **primacy effect** occurs when the earliest information received has a disproportionate influence on the overall impression.

Example: If a person appears unfriendly during a nervous first meeting, the observer may continue interpreting later behaviour through the initial belief that the person is unfriendly.

## Confirmation Bias

**Confirmation bias** occurs when people are more likely to attend to and process information that is consistent with their existing beliefs or initial impressions.

Typical pattern:

1. An initial belief is formed.
2. Supporting information receives attention.
3. Contradictory information is ignored, discarded, or reinterpreted.
4. The original belief becomes stronger.

Recognition cues include selectively seeking supporting evidence, remembering confirming examples, and ignoring contradictory evidence.

## Self-Fulfilling Prophecy

A **self-fulfilling prophecy** occurs when a belief or expectation changes one's actions toward another person, causing that person to behave in a way that confirms the original belief.

Typical sequence:

1. Person A holds a belief about Person B.
2. Person A acts toward Person B on the basis of that belief.
3. Person B responds to Person A's behaviour.
4. Person B's response appears to confirm the original belief.

Example: A teacher expects a student to perform poorly, gives the student fewer opportunities and less useful feedback, and the student's performance eventually declines.

### Confirmation Bias versus Self-Fulfilling Prophecy

| Confirmation Bias | Self-Fulfilling Prophecy |
|---|---|
| Selective attention to or interpretation of existing evidence | Behaviour helps produce a new outcome that confirms an expectation |
| The observer filters information | The observer's treatment changes the other person's behaviour |

# 4. Social Norms, Social Influence, and Conformity

## Social Norms

**Social norms** are prescribed behaviours that vary across context, culture, and time.

Examples include remaining quiet in a library, waiting in line, and following expected behaviour in a restaurant or classroom.

## Social Influence

**Social influence** occurs when thoughts and behaviours are affected by the behaviour of others.

**Normative influence** often causes people to conform because they want approval, acceptance, or freedom from rejection.

## Conformity

**Conformity** is adjusting behaviour or judgment to match a group because of real or imagined group pressure. It does not require a direct command.

## Solomon Asch's Conformity Experiment

### Method

- Participants completed a simple perception task.
- They compared one standard line with three comparison lines.
- Three or more confederates were present.
- The confederates unanimously gave obviously incorrect answers on critical trials.
- The true participant publicly reported an answer.

### Finding

The lecture reports that **75% conformed to an obviously incorrect answer at least once**.

### Conclusion

Group pressure can cause people to contradict their own direct perception. The study demonstrates conformity and normative social influence.

Recognition cue: The individual follows the unanimous group without receiving a direct command from an authority figure.

# 5. Group Cognition

## Groupthink

The lecture defines **groupthink** as faulty decision-making that occurs when a high degree of conformity and group cohesion are highly valued to the exclusion of opposing information and ideas.

Groupthink involves:

- **Overestimating the group**
- **Close-mindedness**
- **Pressure for uniformity**

Historical examples in the lecture include the Challenger space shuttle disaster and Pearl Harbor.

The central problem is not agreement itself. It is the sacrifice of critical evaluation in order to preserve agreement and cohesion.

## Group Polarization

**Group polarization** occurs when group discussion produces a decision that is more extreme than the decisions members would have made alone.

## Common Knowledge Effect

The **common knowledge effect** occurs when group discussion revolves around information that everyone already shares, while important information known by only one or a few members is neglected.

## Diffusion of Responsibility

**Diffusion of responsibility** occurs when individuals feel diminished responsibility for their actions because responsibility is spread across the people present.

# 6. The Bystander Effect

## Bystander Effect

The **bystander effect** occurs when the likelihood that an individual will help decreases as the number of witnesses increases.

The lecture identifies three overarching influences on helping:

1. The level of danger present
2. Whether the perpetrator is present or absent
3. The type of help required

## Diffusion of Responsibility

In an emergency, a bystander may believe that someone else will take responsibility.

Key question: **Is it my responsibility to act?**

## Pluralistic Ignorance

**Pluralistic ignorance** occurs when people use the inaction of others to interpret an ambiguous situation as non-emergency, even though everyone may privately be uncertain.

Typical sequence:

1. A person is uncertain whether the situation is serious.
2. The person observes other bystanders.
3. Other bystanders are also observing and therefore appear calm.
4. Everyone concludes that the situation is probably not serious.

Key question: **Is this actually an emergency?**

### Critical Distinction

- "Someone else will help" indicates diffusion of responsibility.
- "Nobody else looks concerned, so this must not be serious" indicates pluralistic ignorance.

# 7. Obedience to Authority

## Obedience

**Obedience** occurs when an individual follows a direct order from a person in a position of authority.

### Conformity versus Obedience

| Conformity | Obedience |
|---|---|
| Pressure comes from a group | Pressure comes from an authority figure |
| Usually no explicit command | A direct instruction or command is present |
| Asch's study | Milgram's study |

## Stanley Milgram's Obedience Experiment

### Research Question

Milgram studied obedience in the 1960s in response to the atrocities of the Holocaust. He asked whether ordinary people would harm others at the request of an authority figure.

### Method

- Participants believed that they were taking part in a study of memory.
- The participant served as the teacher.
- A confederate served as the learner.
- A shock generator contained 30 toggle switches in 15-volt increments.
- The learner gave scripted responses.
- When the participant hesitated, the experimenter instructed the participant to continue.

### Finding

The lecture states that most participants continued to shock the learner until the highest voltage in obedience to an authority figure.

### Conclusion

Situational factors, especially the presence of a legitimate authority figure, can strongly influence ordinary people to carry out actions that conflict with their values.

### Factors Affecting Obedience

Replication studies varied:

- The location of the study
- The distance between teacher and learner

In general, obedience tends to increase when authority appears more legitimate and immediate. Obedience tends to decrease when the learner is closer and the consequences of the participant's actions are more salient.

### Ethical Concerns

Reported concerns include psychological distress, depression, altered self-image, disturbed sleep, and trauma.

# 8. Social Roles and the Power of the Situation

## Social Roles

**Social roles** are expectations for how individuals occupying particular positions should behave.

## Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment

### Method

- Psychologically healthy young men were recruited.
- Participants were randomly assigned to be prisoners or guards.
- They entered a simulated prison environment.
- The study was intended to last 14 days.

### Findings

- By the second day, powerlessness, dehumanization, and depersonalization escalated.
- Guards displayed abusive behaviour and prisoners experienced severe distress.
- The study ended after six days because of the harmful conditions.

### Conclusion

The experiment illustrates the power of situations and social roles to affect behaviour. Random assignment is important because the participants were not selected to be guards or prisoners on the basis of personality.

### Ethical Concerns and the Belmont Report

The experiment also illustrates unethical scientific practices. The **Belmont Report** identified three core principles for research with human participants:

1. **Respect for persons and informed consent**
2. **Beneficence: do no harm**
3. **Justice: participants should not be exploited**

### Three Classic Studies Compared

| Study | Core Concept | Source of Pressure | Main Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solomon Asch's line study | Conformity | Unanimous group judgment | 75% conformed at least once |
| Stanley Milgram's shock study | Obedience | Direct instruction from authority | Most continued to the highest voltage in the lecture account |
| Philip Zimbardo's prison study | Social roles and situations | Institutional roles and situational structure | Behaviour deteriorated; study ended after six days |

Memory cues:

- Asch: The group is wrong. Will I follow it?
- Milgram: Authority gives an order. Will I obey?
- Zimbardo: A role is assigned. Will behaviour change to fit it?

# 9. Reproduction and Mate Selectivity

## Reproduction

Reproduction is social in almost all animals, and humans are especially social in their approach to reproduction. Human mate selection is shaped by biology, social roles, culture, relationships, and courtship practices.

## Mate Selectivity

Humans are selective in choosing sexual partners. The lecture reports that females tend to be more selective than males and that women may also be more selective than men.

### Biological Explanations

- Sperm are numerous, while eggs are limited.
- Pregnancy carries substantial physical requirements and changes.
- Males can potentially reproduce more continuously.
- Females can reproduce much less frequently.

Higher reproductive investment can increase the cost of a poor mate choice and therefore increase selectivity.

### Sociocultural Explanations

- Women may face greater reputational costs for promiscuity.
- Women may be approached more frequently and therefore have more options.
- Cultural courtship rituals influence selectivity.

### Finkel and Eastwick's Speed-Dating Study

Finkel and Eastwick used a simulated speed-dating event and manipulated whether men or women rotated seats. The study showed that the social role of approaching rather than sex alone can affect selectivity. Observed sex or gender differences should therefore not automatically be attributed entirely to biology.

# 10. Interpersonal Attraction

Factors that increase liking include physical attractiveness, proximity, and similarity. The lecture distinguishes three types of romantic attraction.

## Physical Attraction

Physical attraction is attraction based on appearance and bodily characteristics. It is one of the first features noticed and is especially powerful at the beginning of an interaction.

Walster and colleagues found that physical attractiveness was the only measured attribute that accurately predicted whether university students felt attracted to a potential partner whom they had met only once.

**Beauty is universally beneficial, but beauty is not universal:** attractiveness carries benefits across cultures, but cultures do not necessarily define beauty in the same way.

## Situational Attraction

Situational attraction arises from proximity, repeated contact, shared circumstances, or arousing situations.

### Mere Exposure Effect

The **mere exposure effect** means that repeated exposure and familiarity can increase liking. Spending time with someone may increase attraction, especially when the initial reaction is not strongly negative.

### Dutton and Aron's Capilano Bridge Study

Participants on an arousing suspension bridge experienced physiological arousal. Some interpreted arousal produced by danger as attraction to the interviewer. This demonstrates **misattribution of arousal**: correctly noticing arousal but incorrectly identifying its source.

## Psychological Attraction

Psychological attraction is based on similarity in characteristics such as education, religion, socioeconomic status, personality, attitudes, and values.

### Attraction Comparison

| Type | Main Basis | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Physical attraction | Appearance and bodily characteristics | Attraction during a first meeting |
| Situational attraction | Proximity, familiarity, shared context, or arousal | Attraction among people who repeatedly meet or share an exciting event |
| Psychological attraction | Similarity in personality, values, and background | Attraction based on shared beliefs and goals |

The three forms are not mutually exclusive.

# 11. Motivations for Social Influence

## Hedonic Motivation

The **hedonic principle** states that "all motivation is basically rooted in approach to pleasure and avoidance of pain."

People can be influenced through rewards, punishments, anticipated pleasure, and anticipated pain.

### Overjustification Effect

Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett compared children who received a prize for drawing with children who drew without a prize. The next day, children who had received no prize remained more interested, while rewarded children were less likely to draw.

The **overjustification effect** occurs when an external reward becomes the apparent reason for an activity that was previously intrinsically enjoyable, reducing intrinsic motivation.

## Approval Motivation

**Approval motivation** is the desire to have others like and approve of us. People follow social norms partly because norm-consistent behaviour makes them more socially acceptable.

Approval motivation leads to normative influence and can produce conformity.

### Hedonic versus Approval Motivation

| Hedonic Motivation | Approval Motivation |
|---|---|
| Will this produce pleasure or help me avoid pain? | Will this make other people like or accept me? |

# 12. Aggression

## Definition

The lecture defines **aggression** as "behaviour with the purpose of harming another."

The defining feature is intention. Accidental harm is not aggression, while an unsuccessful attempt to harm can still be aggression.

## Instrumental Aggression

**Instrumental aggression** occurs when harmful or violent behaviour is used on purpose to achieve another goal.

Examples include attacking someone to obtain resources or deliberately knocking down an opponent to win a competition.

## Hostile Aggression

**Hostile aggression** occurs when the purpose of the behaviour is to inflict harm.

The key question is whether harm is the final goal or a means to another goal.

## Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis

The **frustration-aggression hypothesis** proposes that organisms aggress when their desires are frustrated.

Typical sequence:

1. A goal or desire is present.
2. The goal is blocked.
3. Frustration develops.
4. The probability of aggression increases.

The hypothesis is limited because frustration does not always cause aggression, and negative emotional states can produce aggression even when no clear goal has been blocked. Frustration increases the likelihood of aggression but is neither necessary nor sufficient by itself.

## Individual Differences in Aggression

### Biological Factors

- Genetics; the lecture reports that genetics accounts for approximately 60% of variability.
- Sex
- Testosterone levels

The lecture reports that males are significantly more aggressive than females and that testosterone is implicated. Testosterone does not make people aggressive in all situations; it may decrease threat assessment, making individuals less fearful of retaliation.

### Environmental Factors

- Culture
- Societal expectations
- Aggressive immediate family members
- Learned behaviour
- Scarcity of resources

Aggression has decreased significantly over historical time and varies across cultures, showing that it is not inevitable. Resource scarcity can block goals and increase aggression through frustration.

# 13. Prosocial Behaviour, Altruism, and Cooperation

## Prosocial Behaviour

**Prosocial behaviour** is behaviour intended to help, support, or benefit another person. It does not require the helper to receive no benefit.

## Altruism

**Altruism** involves helping another person even when helping imposes a cost and provides no obvious immediate return.

All altruistic behaviour is prosocial, but not every prosocial behaviour is purely altruistic.

## Kinship Selection

The lecture defines **kinship selection** as extending "altruistic" behaviour to related individuals, thereby increasing the likelihood that one's genetic material will be passed on.

Recognition cues: relatives, shared genes, and indirect genetic benefit.

## Reciprocal Altruism

The lecture defines **reciprocal altruism** as extending "altruistic" behaviour with the expectation that the favour will be returned.

Recognition cues: non-relatives may be involved, repeated interactions occur, and future repayment is expected.

## Cooperation

The lecture defines **cooperation** as behaviour by two or more individuals that leads to mutual benefit.

Cooperation can help individuals acquire and protect resources and is adaptive for survival. It is also risky because another individual may exploit, defect, or take benefits without contributing.

### Conceptual Hierarchy

Two unrelated students who repeatedly share notes because each expects future help demonstrate reciprocal altruism and cooperation. The behaviour is also broadly prosocial, but it is not pure altruism because an expected return is present, and it is not kinship selection because the students are not relatives.

# 14. Groups, Prejudice, and Discrimination

## Groups, In-Groups, and Out-Groups

A **group** is a collection of people who have something in common that distinguishes them from others. Groups may include families, teams, religions, ethnicities, and nations. Groups can also be created spontaneously and arbitrarily, such as by shirt colour.

- **In-group:** a group to which a person belongs.
- **Out-group:** a group to which a person does not belong.

People tend to show positive prejudice and positive discrimination toward in-group members, including greater trust, liking, help, and resource allocation.

## Prejudice

The lecture defines **prejudice** as a negative attitude associated with hate. It may be based on gender, ethnicity, religion, country of origin, skin colour, sexual orientation, body size, or other shared characteristics.

Prejudice can be learned from parents, peers, first-hand experience, and vicarious conditioning.

## Vicarious Conditioning

**Vicarious conditioning** is learning an attitude or behaviour by observing how others react and which behaviours are rewarded or punished.

## Discrimination

The lecture defines **discrimination** as adverse behaviour directed at groups toward which one holds negative and prejudicial beliefs. It is acting on prejudice.

### Critical Distinction

| Prejudice | Discrimination |
|---|---|
| Attitude, evaluation, or emotion | Behaviour |
| "I distrust this group." | "I refuse to hire members of this group." |

A person may hold prejudice without overt discrimination, and institutional pressures may produce discriminatory behaviour even when personal prejudice is weak.

# 15. High-Yield Distinctions

1. **Fundamental attribution error versus actor-observer bias:** The first concerns over-attributing other people's behaviour to disposition; the second contrasts situational explanations for self with dispositional explanations for others.
2. **Actor-observer bias versus self-serving bias:** Actor-observer bias depends on who performs the behaviour; self-serving bias depends on whether the outcome is success or failure.
3. **Confirmation bias versus self-fulfilling prophecy:** Confirmation bias filters evidence; a self-fulfilling prophecy changes behaviour and helps create confirming evidence.
4. **Conformity versus obedience:** Conformity comes from group pressure; obedience comes from a direct authority command.
5. **Diffusion of responsibility versus pluralistic ignorance:** The first concerns personal responsibility; the second concerns interpretation of an ambiguous event.
6. **Instrumental versus hostile aggression:** In instrumental aggression harm is a means; in hostile aggression harm is the goal.
7. **Kinship selection versus reciprocal altruism:** Kinship selection benefits shared genes; reciprocal altruism depends on future repayment.
8. **Prejudice versus discrimination:** Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination is behaviour.
