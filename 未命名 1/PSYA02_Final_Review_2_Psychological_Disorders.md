# 1. Psychological Disorders and Abnormal Psychology

## Psychological Disorder

The lecture defines a **psychological disorder** as "a syndrome characterized by clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation or behaviour that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning."

The lecture also describes psychological disorders as patterns of deviant and dysfunctional behaviours, thoughts, or feelings that cause significant distress, may be dangerous, and last for a specified period of time.

Three practical considerations are especially important:

- **Disturbance:** cognition, emotion regulation, or behaviour is disrupted.
- **Distress:** the pattern produces clinically significant suffering.
- **Dysfunction or impairment:** the pattern interferes with school, work, relationships, self-care, or daily life.

Being unusual is not sufficient for diagnosis. A rare interest, a preference for solitude, normal grief, or temporary anxiety before an exam is not automatically a psychological disorder. Intensity, duration, frequency, distress, functional impairment, and exclusion criteria must be considered.

## Categorical and Continuum Approaches

Psychological disorders can be understood categorically and as part of a continuum.

- **Categorical approach:** a person either meets or does not meet diagnostic criteria.
- **Continuum approach:** symptoms range from mild and ordinary to severe and impairing.

Clinical diagnosis may require categories, while symptom severity still varies continuously.

## Abnormal Psychology

The lecture defines **abnormal psychology** as the study of psychological disorders.

### Key Terms

| Term | Definition | Central Question |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | The chronological age or situational period when symptoms first appear | When does it usually begin? |
| Prognosis | The likely course, trajectory, or development of a disorder | What is likely to happen next? |
| Risk factors | Biological, psychological, and social characteristics that increase the likelihood of a disorder | What increases probability? |
| Etiology | The biological, psychological, and/or social causes of a disorder | What causes the disorder? |
| Comorbidities | Other psychological or physical disorders that frequently co-occur | What often appears with it? |
| Remission | A period in which symptoms substantially decrease or disappear | Have symptoms subsided? |

A risk factor raises probability but is neither necessary nor sufficient. It should not be treated as proof of causation.

## Causes of Psychological Disorders

Causal factors may include:

- Genetics
- Biological processes
- Cognitive processes
- Developmental experiences
- Social conditions

Most disorders are not produced by one isolated cause.

# 2. Diagnosis and the DSM-5

## DSM-5

**DSM-5** stands for the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition*. It provides classifications, diagnostic criteria, symptom requirements, duration requirements, and exclusion criteria.

Most DSM disorders share three broad diagnostic requirements:

1. Symptoms cause significant distress or affect functioning.
2. Symptoms cannot be attributed to substance use or another medical condition.
3. Symptoms cannot be better described by another DSM diagnosis.

These common requirements do not replace disorder-specific criteria. They prevent clinicians from diagnosing on the basis of one symptom when another medical, substance-related, or psychological explanation fits better.

# 3. Anxiety Disorders

## Fear and Anxiety

Anxiety disorders are a group of disorders marked by excessive fear and anxiety.

- **Fear** is generally directed toward an immediate, concrete threat.
- **Anxiety** generally concerns anticipated, uncertain, or future threat.

Fear and anxiety are adaptive reactions to threats. They become maladaptive when they are excessive, difficult to control, disproportionate to actual danger, and disruptive to normal functioning.

The lecture reports that the DSM-5 recognizes 12 anxiety disorders and focuses on generalized anxiety disorder, phobic disorders, and panic disorder.

## Generalized Anxiety Disorder

The lecture defines **generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)** as an anxiety disorder in which worries are not focused on any specific threat.

### Core Features

- Worry is generalized across multiple events or domains.
- Worry occurs more days than not for at least six months.
- The individual finds it difficult to control the worry.
- Worry produces distress or functional impairment.

### Diagnostic Criteria Presented in the Lecture

1. Excessive anxiety and worry about more than one event or stressor, occurring more days than not for at least six months.
2. Difficulty controlling the worry.
3. Three or more of the following:
   - Restlessness
   - Fatigue
   - Concentration deficiency
   - Irritability
   - Muscle tension
   - Sleep disturbance
4. Significant distress or impaired functioning.
5. Not attributable to substance use or another medical condition.
6. Not better described by another DSM diagnosis.

Memory cue: **many topics, most days, six months**.

### Onset and Prognosis

- Onset rarely occurs before adolescence.
- Median age at diagnosis is approximately 30.
- Many patients report symptoms long before seeking help or receiving a diagnosis.
- Anxiety content changes across the lifespan.
- For an individual, symptom severity waxes and wanes.
- Full remission is rare.

## Phobic Disorders

A **phobic disorder** is characterized by marked, persistent, excessive fear of specific objects, activities, or situations. The individual commonly recognizes that the fear is irrational or excessive but cannot control it.

### Specific Phobia

A **specific phobia** is maladaptive fear directed toward a particular object or non-social situation.

Examples presented in the lecture include:

- Animals: dogs, cats, spiders, snakes
- Natural environments: earthquakes, darkness
- Situations: elevators, enclosed spaces
- Medical events: blood, injections, injury
- Other stimuli: loud noises, costumed characters, choking

### Social Phobia

The lecture defines **social phobia** as maladaptive fear of being publicly humiliated or embarrassed. It is also commonly called social anxiety disorder.

Examples include fear of public speaking, being observed, eating in front of others, interacting with strangers, or showing visible anxiety symptoms.

### Specific versus Social Phobia

| Specific Phobia | Social Phobia |
|---|---|
| Fear concerns a concrete object or non-social situation | Fear concerns negative social evaluation, humiliation, or embarrassment |
| Example: fear of spiders or enclosed spaces | Example: fear of being mocked during a presentation |

The same setting can reflect different disorders depending on the feared consequence. Fear of falling from a stage suggests a specific situational fear; fear of being judged while speaking suggests social phobia.

## Preparedness Theory

**Preparedness theory** proposes that organisms may be evolutionarily adapted to fear certain types of stimuli.

The lecture's evidence comes from conditioning: monkeys can easily be conditioned to fear snakes but not flowers. The theory does not claim that everyone is born with a fixed phobia. It proposes that fear of evolutionarily relevant stimuli may be learned more easily and may resist extinction.

Preparedness theory is narrower than the biopsychosocial model. It explains why some fear associations are especially easy to acquire; the biopsychosocial model explains disorder risk through interacting biological, psychological, and social factors.

## Panic Attack

A **panic attack** is a sudden occurrence of multiple psychological and physical symptoms associated with terror.

Symptoms in the lecture include:

- Shortness of breath
- Heart palpitations
- Sweating
- Dizziness
- Derealization, or the feeling that the world is unreal
- Fear of death
- Fear of losing one's mind

The lecture reports that approximately one-third of Canadians experience a panic attack one or more times per year, often during extreme stress. A single or occasional panic attack is not sufficient for panic disorder.

## Panic Disorder

**Panic disorder** requires recurrent, unexpected panic attacks and significant fear of another attack. The individual may change behaviour or avoid situations because of anticipated attacks.

Critical distinction:

- A panic attack is an event.
- Panic disorder is a diagnosis involving recurrent, unexpected attacks and continued concern about recurrence.

## Agoraphobia

**Agoraphobia** is fear of situations from which escape may be difficult or in which help may be unavailable if panic-like symptoms occur. Examples may include public transportation, crowds, open spaces, being far from home, or being alone outside the home.

Agoraphobia is not simply a literal fear of public squares. It may co-occur with panic disorder, but the concepts are not identical.

## Anxiety Disorder Comparison

| Feature | GAD | Phobic Disorder | Panic Disorder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core experience | Chronic, broad worry | Excessive fear of a specific object or situation | Sudden, intense episodes of terror |
| Threat focus | Multiple life domains | Clear and specific | Attacks are recurrent and unexpected |
| Time pattern | More days than not for at least six months | Triggered by contact or anticipated contact | Sudden attacks followed by concern about recurrence |
| Typical cognition | "Many things may go wrong." | "This object or situation is dangerous." | "I am dying or losing control; it may happen again." |

# 4. Mood Disorders

## Mood

The lecture describes **moods** as emotional states that are long-lasting, non-specific, and less intense than many emotions.

Mood disorders are psychological disorders in which mood disturbance is the prominent feature.

## Depressive and Manic Episodes

- **Depressive episode:** a distinct period of sad mood and loss of interest or pleasure.
- **Manic episode:** a distinct period of high energy and increased activity.

Depressive disorders include feelings of sadness, emptiness, hopelessness, and irritability. Symptoms are long-lasting and affect many areas of life.

# 5. Major Depressive Disorder

## Definition

**Major depressive disorder (MDD)** is also called unipolar depression. The lecture describes it as severely depressed mood and/or inability to experience pleasure lasting two or more weeks, accompanied by symptoms such as worthlessness, lethargy, sleep disturbance, and appetite disturbance.

## Diagnostic Criteria Presented in the Lecture

Five or more of the following must be present during the same two-week period:

1. Depressed mood
2. Diminished interest
3. Significant weight loss or gain
4. Insomnia or hypersomnia
5. Psychomotor agitation or retardation
6. Fatigue
7. Feelings of worthlessness or guilt
8. Diminished concentration or decisiveness
9. Recurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation

Additional criteria:

- No evidence of a manic episode.
- Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment.
- Symptoms are not better described by another DSM disorder.
- Symptoms are not attributable to another medical condition or the physiological effects of substance use.

### Psychomotor Terms

- **Psychomotor agitation:** visibly increased movement, pacing, inability to sit still, or marked physical restlessness.
- **Psychomotor retardation:** slowed movement, speech, and thought.

## Onset

MDD may appear at any age but is most likely to appear in the twenties.

## Prognosis

The lecture reports:

- Two-fifths recover within three months.
- Four-fifths recover within one year.
- One-fifth do not experience remission.

Recovery from one episode does not guarantee that no future episode will occur.

## Risk Factors

### Temperamental

- Neuroticism
- Negative affect

### Environmental

- Childhood experiences
- Stressful life events

### Biological

- Neurotransmitter imbalance

### Genetic

- Family members of people with MDD are two to four times more likely to be diagnosed.
- The lecture reports approximately 40% heritability.

Heritability is a population-level estimate of variation associated with genetic differences. It does not mean that 40% of one person's disorder is caused by genes.

## Comorbidities

The lecture lists:

- Substance-related disorders
- Panic disorders
- Obsessive-compulsive disorder
- Anorexia nervosa
- Bulimia nervosa

Comorbidity means frequent co-occurrence, not that every individual with MDD has these conditions.

# 6. Helplessness Theory

The lecture states that **helplessness theory** argues that the way a person thinks about failure makes that person more or less likely to be depressed.

A helpless attributional pattern interprets failure as:

- **Internal:** caused by a personal defect.
- **Stable:** permanent and unlikely to change.
- **Global:** applicable to many areas of life.

Example after an examination failure:

| Dimension | Helpless Attribution | Healthier Attribution |
|---|---|---|
| Internal versus external | "I am unintelligent." | "My study strategy did not fit this examination." |
| Stable versus mutable | "I will never improve." | "I can change my preparation." |
| Global versus local | "I fail at everything." | "This result concerns one examination." |

The highest-risk pattern is **internal + stable + global**.

Helplessness theory connects with the ABC model in treatment: an event does not determine consequences by itself; beliefs about the event influence emotional and behavioural outcomes.

# 7. Dysthymia and Double Depression

## Dysthymia

The lecture describes **dysthymia**, or dysthymic disorder, as moderate depressive symptoms lasting more than two years. It is commonly called persistent depressive disorder in current terminology.

The symptoms may be less severe than a major depressive episode but are much more persistent.

## Double Depression

**Double depression** occurs when dysthymia is punctuated by episodes of major depression.

It is not simply two major depressive episodes. It requires a chronic baseline of moderate depression plus superimposed major depressive episodes.

### Time-Course Comparison

| Disorder | Severity | Time Pattern | Best Recognition Cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| MDD | Severe | A major depressive episode lasting at least two weeks | Five of nine symptoms |
| Dysthymia | Usually moderate | Continuous for more than two years | Chronic but comparatively less severe depression |
| Double depression | Chronic moderate symptoms plus severe episodes | Major episodes occur on top of dysthymia | Persistent baseline plus episodic major depression |

# 8. Bipolar Disorders

## General Definition

Bipolar disorders are characterized by cycles of abnormal, persistent high mood and low mood. Ordinary mood fluctuation is not sufficient; mania or hypomania must be present.

## Bipolar I Disorder

**Bipolar I disorder** requires at least one manic episode. Hypomanic and depressive episodes may also occur, but a depressive episode is not required for the defining Bipolar I criterion presented in the lecture.

## Manic Episode

The lecture describes a manic episode as a distinct period of abnormal, persistently elevated mood and increased activity or energy lasting at least one week.

Three or more of the following are required:

- Inflated self-esteem
- Decreased need for sleep
- Increased talkativeness
- Racing thoughts
- Distractibility
- Increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation
- Excessive involvement in activities with high potential for painful consequences

Common DSM requirements also apply: significant distress or impairment, no better explanation by another diagnosis, and no attribution to a medical condition or substance.

**Decreased need for sleep** differs from insomnia. In insomnia, the person wants to sleep and often feels tired. During mania, a person may sleep very little and still feel energetic.

## Bipolar II Disorder

**Bipolar II disorder** involves both hypomanic and depressive episodes but no full manic episode.

Hypomania resembles mania but is less severe. Bipolar II should not be assumed to be harmless or globally mild because its depressive episodes may cause serious impairment.

## Bipolar I versus Bipolar II

| Bipolar I | Bipolar II |
|---|---|
| At least one full manic episode | Hypomania and depression, with no full manic episode |
| Depressive episodes may occur but are not the defining requirement | Depressive episodes are part of the pattern |
| Mania produces more severe impairment | Elevated episodes are less severe than full mania |

## Bipolar I Prevalence, Onset, and Prognosis

The lecture reports:

- Prevalence of approximately one in 40.
- Approximately equal prevalence in women and men.
- Mean age of first episode: 18 years.
- First onset may still occur in the sixties or seventies.
- Approximately 90% of people who experience a manic episode will experience more throughout life.
- Full remission is rare.

## Bipolar I Risk Factors

### Genetic

- Bipolar disorder is among the most heritable disorders.
- Concordance among identical twins is reported as 40% to 70%.

Concordance below 100% shows that genetic risk is important but not sufficient.

### Environmental

- High stress
- Highly emotionally expressive family members
- Separation or divorce

### Psychological

- High neuroticism
- High conscientiousness

## Bipolar I Comorbidities

- Anxiety disorders
- Substance use disorders
- Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder
- Behavioural disorders

## MDD versus Bipolar Disorder

| MDD | Bipolar Disorder |
|---|---|
| Depressive episodes without mania | Mania or hypomania is present |
| Unipolar | Includes abnormal elevated states |
| A history of mania rules out uncomplicated MDD | Bipolar I requires mania; Bipolar II requires hypomania and depression |

When evaluating a current depressive episode, always ask whether the person has ever experienced mania or hypomania.

# 9. Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders

## Obsessions

The lecture defines **obsessions** as recurrent, unwanted, and intrusive thoughts, fears, urges, or images.

Example: an intrusive fear of germs or contamination.

## Compulsions

The lecture defines **compulsions** as behaviours performed in response to an obsession in an attempt to reduce associated anxiety.

Example: repetitive handwashing in response to contamination fears.

A compulsion may be an observable behaviour or a mental act such as counting or silently repeating words.

Typical cycle:

1. Obsession produces anxiety.
2. Compulsion temporarily reduces anxiety.
3. Temporary relief reinforces the compulsion.
4. The cycle continues.

## Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)** involves obsessions, compulsions, or both, with significant distress or impairment.

OCD was previously categorized as an anxiety disorder but is now grouped under obsessive-compulsive and related disorders.

### Risk Factors Presented in the Lecture

- Strong genetic vulnerability in twin studies
- Head trauma
- Brain inflammation caused by encephalitis
- Seizure disorder
- Birth complications
- Childhood strep throat
- Lower levels of serotonin

These are risk factors, not features required in every case.

## Hoarding Disorder

**Hoarding disorder** involves difficulty discarding useless possessions, leading to excessive accumulation.

Consequences may include:

- Living spaces becoming unusable
- Sanitation concerns
- Fire hazards
- Tripping hazards
- Health-code violations
- Isolation, conflict, and loneliness

The distinction from ordinary collecting is impairment: organized collecting does not normally make living areas unusable or dangerous.

# 10. Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders

## Trauma and Stress

- **Trauma:** an emotional response to something shocking, terrifying, dangerous, or life-threatening.
- **Stress:** a reaction to life demands.

## Posttraumatic Stress Disorder

**Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD)** may follow experiencing or witnessing a traumatic event such as war, natural disaster, violent crime, assault, or a serious accident.

The lecture reports that symptoms usually begin within three months of exposure. It reports current prevalence of approximately 3.5% and lifetime prevalence approaching 9%.

### Four Symptom Categories

1. **Re-experiencing symptoms:** intrusive memories, nightmares, flashbacks, or intense distress in response to reminders.
2. **Avoidance symptoms:** avoiding thoughts, feelings, people, places, activities, or conversations associated with the trauma.
3. **Cognitive and mood symptoms:** negative beliefs, blame, guilt, detachment, reduced interest, diminished positive emotion, or inability to recall parts of the event.
4. **Arousal and reactivity symptoms:** hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, irritability, sleep disturbance, concentration problems, or reckless behaviour.

Experiencing trauma alone does not establish PTSD. The characteristic symptom pattern and functional impact are required.

# 11. Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders

## General Definition

These disorders involve behaviours that stimulate the brain's reward system, release dopamine, and produce addiction. They may involve substances or behaviours.

The lecture defines **addiction** as repeated and compulsive engagement in rewarding activities with disregard for negative long-term outcomes.

## Substance Use and Substance-Induced Disorders

- **Substance use disorders:** problematic patterns of substance use.
- **Substance-induced disorders:** states directly produced by substances, such as intoxication, withdrawal, or substance-induced psychological symptoms.

## Physiological Dependence and Tolerance

- **Physiological dependence:** the body adapts to a substance and may produce withdrawal when use stops.
- **Tolerance:** progressively larger amounts are needed to achieve the same effect.

## Gambling Disorder

**Gambling disorder** is a behavioural addiction involving repeated betting, pursuit of euphoria, and an uncontrollable urge to continue despite consequences.

The lecture reports:

- Prevalence of approximately 0.3%.
- More frequent diagnosis in men.

Occasional gambling is not sufficient. The critical pattern is compulsive continuation despite financial, occupational, interpersonal, or other serious harm.

# 12. Neurocognitive Disorders

## General Definition

**Neurocognitive disorders** involve problems with how the brain processes information because of underlying neurological conditions.

The most important defining feature is that they are **acquired**: the cognitive deficit was not present at birth.

Potential causes include:

- Brain injury
- Disease
- Substance or medication use

Affected functions may include memory, attention, language, reasoning, executive function, and perception.

## Alzheimer's Disease

**Alzheimer's disease** is a neurocognitive disorder involving progressive neurological change and acquired decline in memory and other cognitive functions. Over time, the decline interferes with independent daily functioning.

Older age alone is not a diagnosis. The central feature is acquired, clinically meaningful cognitive decline.

# 13. Personality Disorders

## General Definition

The lecture describes **personality disorders** as pervasive and enduring patterns of characteristics, beliefs, and behaviours that cause distress because they differ drastically from societal expectations.

Core features include:

- Pervasive across multiple contexts
- Enduring over time
- Inflexible
- Distressing or impairing
- Usually first visible during adolescence or early adulthood

Unlike an episodic mood disorder, a personality disorder is a stable, cross-situational pattern.

## Personality Disorder Clusters

- **Cluster A:** odd and eccentric behaviours
- **Cluster B:** dramatic, emotional, and erratic behaviours
- **Cluster C:** anxious and fearful behaviours

## Borderline Personality Disorder

**Borderline personality disorder (BPD)** belongs to Cluster B.

The lecture emphasizes:

- Instability in interpersonal relationships
- Instability in self-image
- Instability in emotion
- Intense and rapidly changing moods
- Black-and-white or all-good/all-bad thinking
- Impulsive harmful behaviours used to self-soothe
- Substance use, risky sexual encounters, excessive spending, self-injury, and suicidal behaviour
- Rapid life changes made without preparation or consideration of consequences

The best summary is persistent instability in relationships, self-image, and emotion, accompanied by impulsivity and all-or-nothing thinking.

One mood change or one impulsive action is not sufficient for diagnosis; the pattern must be pervasive, enduring, and impairing.

# 14. The Biopsychosocial Model

The **biopsychosocial model** states that many factors and events contribute to psychological disorders and that interactions determine risk.

## Biological Factors

- Genetics
- Brain structure and function
- Neurotransmitters
- Hormones
- Physical illness
- Brain injury

## Psychological Factors

- Cognitive style
- Helpless attribution
- Coping strategies
- Learned fear
- Personality traits
- Traumatic experiences

## Social Factors

- Family environment
- Social support
- Poverty
- Discrimination
- Cultural expectations
- Relationship conflict
- Stressful life events

The model emphasizes interaction rather than a list of independent causes.

Example for depression:

- Biological vulnerability: family history and neurotransmitter functioning
- Psychological vulnerability: internal, stable, and global interpretation of failure
- Social stress: relationship loss, chronic stress, and weak social support

No single factor must be sufficient, but the combination can substantially increase risk.
