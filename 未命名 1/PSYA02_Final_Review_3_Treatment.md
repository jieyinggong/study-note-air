# 2. Why Treatment Matters

Psychological disorders create personal, social, and financial costs.

## Personal and Social Costs

- Inability to carry out daily activities
- Difficulty managing relationships
- Reduced ability to work or study
- Reduced quality of life
- Burden on families, caregivers, and communities

The lecture emphasizes that impairment associated with psychological disorders can be as severe as impairment associated with physical illness.

## Financial Cost

The lecture reports approximately **$51 billion per year in lost work**.

# 3. Access to Treatment

## Access Statistics Presented in the Lecture

- Approximately 20% of Canadians experience a moderate to serious mental health disorder at some point in life.
- Only approximately 40% of those individuals seek treatment.
- Approximately one-third of those who seek treatment are dissatisfied with the level of treatment received.

Seeking treatment and obtaining accessible, appropriate, satisfactory treatment are separate problems.

## Why People Do Not Receive Treatment

### Failure to Recognize a Disorder

People may not realize that they have a disorder. Mental illness is often less well understood than physical illness, and symptoms may be mistaken for ordinary stress or personal weakness.

### Psychological Beliefs

The lecture reports:

- 72.6% believe they can treat themselves.
- 16.4% believe the problem is not severe.
- 9.1% believe that they will be stigmatized for seeking mental health treatment.

**Stigma** is a negative social label, evaluation, or discriminatory response attached to mental illness or treatment seeking.

### Structural Barriers

- Not knowing where to look
- Too few clinicians
- Cost
- Long waits
- Limited insurance coverage

The lecture notes that OHIP does not cover psychologists, counsellors, or social workers and that private insurance may impose low limits. University of Toronto student benefits may provide additional support.

# 5. Psychodynamic Therapy and Psychoanalysis

## Origins

Psychodynamic therapy has roots in Sigmund Freud's methods of **psychoanalysis**.

## Core Assumptions

Psychological problems may arise from:

- Ineffective repression of urges, often beginning in childhood
- Fixations
- Problematic use of defence mechanisms
- Conflict between parts of the dynamic unconscious
- Conflict between "what I want to do" and "what I should do"

## Goal

The central goal is **insight**: conscious awareness and understanding of unconscious thoughts, feelings, behaviours, motives, and conflicts.

## Common Topics and Techniques

- Childhood events
- Dream analysis
- Subconscious thoughts and urges
- Projective techniques
- Interpretation of unconscious conflict

Recognition cues: unconscious conflict, childhood, repression, defence mechanisms, dream analysis, and insight.

# 6. Humanistic and Existential Therapies

## Humanistic Assumptions

Humanistic theory emphasizes:

- Striving for personal improvement
- Free will
- Positive aspects of human experience
- Capacity for growth

The goal is to help patients develop a stronger and healthier sense of identity.

## Difference from Psychodynamic Therapy

| Psychodynamic Therapy | Humanistic Therapy |
|---|---|
| Emphasizes unconscious conflict and childhood experience | Emphasizes free will, growth, and positive potential |
| Therapist helps interpret hidden conflicts | Therapist provides a relationship that supports self-directed growth |
| Insight into the unconscious is central | Development of a healthier identity is central |

## Carl Rogers and Person-Centred Therapy

**Person-centred therapy** assumes that individuals have a tendency toward growth. It centres on acceptance and genuine reactions from the therapist.

### Congruence

**Congruence** means that the therapist is genuine and that words, body language, emotional expression, and inner attitude are consistent.

### Empathy

**Empathy** means understanding the client's experience from the client's point of view.

### Unconditional Positive Regard

**Unconditional positive regard** means accepting the basic worth of the client without making that acceptance dependent on perfect behaviour.

It does not require approval of every action. The therapist can challenge a harmful behaviour while continuing to value and accept the person.

Memory cue: **genuineness, understanding, and acceptance**.

---

# 7. Behaviour Therapy

## Behaviourism

Behaviour therapy relies on **behaviourism**, the study of observable and measurable variables. It focuses on changing actions to reduce psychological problems.

The underlying assumption is that maladaptive behaviour may be learned and can therefore be modified through new learning.

## Operant Conditioning

**Operant conditioning** changes behaviour through its consequences.

- Reinforcement or reward increases behaviour.
- Punishment decreases behaviour.

### Token Economy

A **token economy** rewards target behaviour with tokens, vouchers, points, or objects that can later be exchanged for desired rewards.

Example: A patient receives tokens for completing self-care routines and exchanges them for privileges.

## Classical Conditioning

**Classical conditioning** changes learned associations between stimuli.

### Exposure Therapy

The lecture describes **exposure therapy** as harmless, repeated exposure to a stimulus believed to be threatening, leading to a reduction in the threat response.

Exposure may proceed gradually, for example:

1. Reading the word "spider"
2. Viewing a picture
3. Viewing a video
4. Observing a real spider from a distance
5. Approaching safely

Exposure is not simply uncontrolled confrontation. It is structured, repeated contact in a safe context that allows the learned fear response to decrease.

# 8. Cognitive Therapy

## Core Assumption

Where behaviour therapy works to change actions, **cognitive therapy** works to change unhealthy thought patterns that contribute to psychological problems.

Cognitive therapists focus on restructuring irrational negative thought processes into more rational and evidence-based ones.

Targets include:

- Automatic beliefs
- Assumptions
- Predictions
- Irrational thoughts
- Negative interpretations

## Cognitive Restructuring

**Cognitive restructuring** teaches clients to identify, question, test, and revise automatic beliefs and predictions that produce unhealthy emotion.

Example belief: "I will never be able to succeed in school."

Evidence-testing questions:

- What successes have occurred in the past?
- Is one failure evidence about every future outcome?
- What alternative explanations fit the evidence?
- Is the belief absolute or exaggerated?

A revised belief is not forced positivity. It is a more accurate and flexible interpretation, such as: "I struggled on this assessment, but I have succeeded before and can change my preparation."

---

# 9. Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy

**Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT)** combines cognitive and behavioural orientations.

It addresses interactions among:

- Thoughts
- Emotions
- Behaviours

Changing one component can influence the others.

The lecture identifies CBT as the most common psychological treatment for depression and anxiety and describes it as:

- Problem focused
- Action oriented
- Transparent

Transparency means that clients understand the techniques, goals, and rationale of treatment.

CBT may combine cognitive restructuring with behavioural practice, exposure, activity scheduling, or other action-based techniques.

---

# 10. The ABC Model

The **ABC model** is a short-term, action-oriented, problem-focused approach used in cognitive restructuring.

## A: Activating Event

What happened?

Example: A student failed an examination.

## B: Beliefs

How does the person interpret the activating event?

Example: "One failure proves that I am unintelligent and will never succeed."

## C: Consequences

What emotional and behavioural consequences follow the belief?

Example: hopelessness, withdrawal, and refusal to prepare for the next assessment.

## Core Relationship

The activating event does not directly and completely determine the consequence. Beliefs mediate the relationship:

**A -> B -> C**

The same activating event can produce different consequences through different beliefs.

### Unhealthy Path

- A: A relationship ends.
- B: "No one will ever love me, and every relationship will fail."
- C: Severe hopelessness and complete withdrawal.

### Healthier Path

- A: The same relationship ends.
- B: "This is painful, but it does not define my worth or every future relationship."
- C: Sadness accompanied by support seeking and gradual recovery.

Rational belief does not remove every negative emotion. Sadness after a loss can be a healthy negative emotion. Cognitive restructuring aims to reduce unhealthy consequences produced by irrational, absolute, or inaccurate beliefs.

The ABC model connects to helplessness theory: internal, stable, and global interpretations of failure can form the B that produces depressive consequences.

# 12. Biological Treatments

## General Definition

Biological treatments use therapeutic techniques to change an individual's physiology.

They include:

- Medication
- Electroconvulsive therapy
- Transcranial magnetic stimulation
- Psychosurgery
- Herbal or natural products

**Psychopharmacotherapy** is treatment of psychological illness with medication.

# 13. Antipsychotic Medication

## Chlorpromazine

Chlorpromazine was initially developed in France as an antihistamine. It also made patients sleepy and was used as an anaesthetic. Its calming effects contributed to its use as an antipsychotic medication and changed the treatment of schizophrenia.

## Mechanism

Chlorpromazine blocks **dopamine receptors**.

Dopamine is associated with:

- Pleasure
- Motivation
- Motor control

Blocking dopamine receptors can reduce some psychotic symptoms but can also disrupt movement.

## Side Effects

### Extrapyramidal Symptoms

**Extrapyramidal symptoms** include abnormal movement effects such as rigidity, tremor, slowed movement, restlessness, and involuntary movement.

### Tardive Dyskinesia

**Tardive dyskinesia** is a potentially persistent pattern of repetitive involuntary movements, often affecting the face, mouth, tongue, or limbs after prolonged exposure to some antipsychotic medications.

Mechanism link:

**Dopamine blockade -> reduced psychotic symptoms, but possible disruption of motor control.**

---

# 14. Anti-Anxiety Medication

## Benzodiazepines

The lecture focuses on **benzodiazepines**, such as diazepam. They may be prescribed for panic disorders and other severe anxiety symptoms.

## GABA Mechanism

**GABA** stands for gamma-aminobutyric acid and is a major inhibitory neurotransmitter.

Benzodiazepines facilitate GABA activity, inhibit excessive neural activity, and reduce anxiety.

Mechanism cue:

**Benzodiazepine -> increased GABA action -> neural inhibition -> reduced anxiety.**

## Limitations

- Drug tolerance
- Withdrawal symptoms
- Drowsiness
- Poor coordination
- High addiction potential

The medications may act quickly, but tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, and addiction limit long-term use.

# 15. Antidepressant Medication

## Monoamine Neurotransmitters

The lecture discusses three monoamine neurotransmitters:

- Serotonin
- Norepinephrine
- Dopamine

The course presents depression as related to imbalance in these brain chemicals. This is a simplified biological account and should be integrated with psychological and social factors.

## Synaptic Transmission

After a neurotransmitter is released into the synaptic cleft, it may be:

- Taken back into the presynaptic neuron through reuptake
- Broken down by enzymes

Antidepressants can increase neurotransmitter availability by interfering with breakdown or reuptake.

## Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors

**Monoamine oxidase (MAO)** is an enzyme that breaks down monoamines, including serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine.

**Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)** inhibit this enzyme, so monoamine neurotransmitters remain available for longer.

MAOIs were discovered when medication used to treat tuberculosis unexpectedly elevated mood.

Limitations in the lecture include:

- Dizziness
- Loss of sexual interest
- Dangerous and potentially lethal food and drug interactions

## Reuptake Inhibitors

**Reuptake inhibitors** prevent neurotransmitters from being taken back up into the presynaptic neuron. This increases neurotransmitter concentration in the synaptic space.

### Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors

**SSRIs** selectively prevent serotonin reuptake, making more serotonin available at the synapse. Cipralex is the example in the lecture.

### Medications Affecting Serotonin and Norepinephrine

Other antidepressants affect more than one neurotransmitter, including serotonin and norepinephrine.

The lecture places tricyclic antidepressants and Effexor together when discussing action on serotonin and norepinephrine. For examination purposes, retain the lecture's central mechanism distinction:

- SSRIs primarily increase serotonin availability.
- Other antidepressants act on serotonin and norepinephrine.

In standard pharmacological classification, Effexor, or venlafaxine, is usually classified as an SNRI rather than a tricyclic antidepressant.

## MAOIs versus Reuptake Inhibitors

| MAOIs | Reuptake Inhibitors |
|---|---|
| Prevent enzymatic breakdown of monoamines | Prevent neurotransmitters from being reabsorbed |
| Target breakdown | Target reuptake |
| Increase neurotransmitter availability | Increase neurotransmitter availability |

# 16. Electroconvulsive Therapy

## Procedure

**Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** involves brief electrical stimulation of the brain while the patient is under anaesthesia. The stimulation induces controlled seizures.

## Uses

ECT is most commonly used for severe major depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia that has not responded to other treatments.

## Benefits

- Highly effective and safe in modern practice
- The lecture reports effectiveness for approximately 50% to 70% of people

## Limitations

- Effects are not necessarily permanent
- Treatment may need to be repeated
- Confusion may occur
- Short-term memory loss may occur
- The lecture states that confusion and short-term memory effects usually resolve after 7 to 14 days

Recognition cue: **anaesthesia + brief electrical stimulation + controlled seizure**.

---

# 17. Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation

**Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)** is a non-invasive procedure that uses small magnetic pulses to stimulate nerve cells in the brain and improve symptoms of major depression.

Features in the lecture:

- Pulses may feel like light taps on the head.
- Pulses pass through the skull and approximately one inch into the brain.
- They can increase or decrease neuronal activity.
- TMS is used when other treatments have not been effective.
- It is considered non-invasive.
- It may produce longer-lasting changes to brain chemistry.

Recognition cue: **external magnetic pulses + non-invasive**.

---

# 18. Psychosurgery and Deep Brain Stimulation

## Psychosurgery

**Psychosurgery** is an operation in which psychological disorders are addressed using surgical procedures. Some forms remove or destroy small areas of the brain believed to contribute to mental dysfunction.

Historical examples include:

- **Trepanation:** an early procedure involving an opening in the skull.
- **Prefrontal lobotomy:** severing connections between the prefrontal lobe and the rest of the brain to treat severe psychosis.

Prefrontal lobotomy can produce severe irreversible cognitive and personality effects and is not a routine modern treatment.

## Deep Brain Stimulation

**Deep brain stimulation (DBS)** is an invasive surgical intervention in which electrodes are embedded in the brain to produce repetitive stimulation.

The electrical current can help prevent or regulate signals associated with psychiatric symptoms.

Features:

- Invasive
- Requires brain surgery
- Uses implanted electrodes
- Carries elevated risk
- Generally reserved as a last resort for treatment-resistant mental illness

Recognition cue: **surgically implanted electrodes + invasive + last resort**.

| Feature | ECT | TMS | DBS |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stimulation | Brief electrical stimulation producing a controlled seizure | External magnetic pulses | Electrical stimulation through implanted electrodes |
| Anaesthesia or surgery | Anaesthesia | Generally no surgery | Brain surgery |
| Invasiveness | No implanted brain device, but controlled seizure under anaesthesia | Non-invasive | Invasive |
