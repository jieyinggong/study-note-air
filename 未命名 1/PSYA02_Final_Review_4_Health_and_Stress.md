# 1. Stress and Stressors

## Stress

The lecture defines **stress** as "the physical and psychological responses to internal or external stressors."

Stress is therefore the response, not the event itself. The response may include thoughts, emotions, changes in attention, hormonal activity, autonomic activity, and immune-system activity.

## Stressors

The lecture defines **stressors** as "internal or external circumstances and/or stimuli perceived as a threat, challenge or deviation from a balanced state."

A stressor may be:

- Internal, such as pain, illness, worry, or a distressing memory
- External, such as an examination, conflict, noise, poverty, or a natural disaster

The word **perceived** is essential. The same event may produce different stress responses in different people because people appraise its demands and their available resources differently.

## Critical Distinction

| Stressor | Stress |
|---|---|
| A circumstance or stimulus | The physical and psychological response |
| May be internal or external | Occurs in the person responding |
| Example: an examination | Example: worry, arousal, and increased heart rate |

# 2. Good Stress and Bad Stress

## Hans Selye

**Hans Selye** is described in the lecture as the "Father of Stress Research." He coined the term **stress**.

## Eustress

**Eustress** is a reaction to good stress. It can accompany meaningful challenges, excitement, growth, or demanding goals that a person believes can be managed.

## Distress

**Distress** is a reaction to bad stress. It occurs when demands feel harmful, overwhelming, or beyond the person's ability to cope.

Eustress and distress are reactions, not simply fixed properties of an event. Appraisal helps determine whether a potential stressor is experienced as a manageable challenge or an overwhelming threat.

# 3. How Much Stress Is Optimal?

## Yerkes-Dodson Law

The **Yerkes-Dodson Law** proposes that performance changes with stress or arousal:

- Too little stress leads to underperformance.
- Moderate stress produces peak performance.
- When stress becomes too high, performance declines.

The central recognition cue is an inverted-U relation: both underload and overload may impair performance, while a moderate level is optimal.

## Maximal Adaptability Model

The **Maximal Adaptability Model** challenges the Yerkes-Dodson Law. It emphasizes that humans are highly adaptable to stress and that high performance can sometimes be maintained under both underload and overload.

## Comparison

| Model | Central Claim |
|---|---|
| Yerkes-Dodson Law | Moderate stress is associated with peak performance; very low or very high stress reduces performance. |
| Maximal Adaptability Model | Humans can adapt, allowing high performance to be maintained across underload and overload. |

Do not treat the Yerkes-Dodson Law as claiming that all stress is harmful. It explicitly predicts that too little stress can also impair performance.

# 4. Types of Stressors

The lecture distinguishes stressors according to **duration** and **severity**.

| Type of Stressor | Duration | Severity | Lecture Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute stressor | Short-term circumstance lasting minutes to hours | May be mild, moderate, or severe | Childbirth, divorce, taking an exam, public speaking |
| Chronic stressor | Enduring circumstance lasting weeks to years | May be mild, moderate, or severe | Living in poverty |
| Traumatic stressor | Immediate threat to physical integrity | The most severe type | Child abuse, natural disasters |

## Exam Notes

- **Acute** refers primarily to short duration, not necessarily low severity.
- **Chronic** refers to persistence over time, not necessarily constant maximum intensity.
- **Traumatic** emphasizes an immediate threat to physical integrity and the greatest severity.
- A major life event can be acute even when its effects continue after the event. Classification depends on how the stressor is described in the question.

# 5. The Stress Response: An Evolutionary Perspective

The lecture defines **stress responses** as "integrated psychological and biological responses to stressors that help restore a balanced state."

Stress responses are highly coordinated processes that:

- Protect the body from harm
- Mobilize energy to cope with stress
- Help prevent injury
- Reduce infection risk

## Homeostasis

**Homeostasis** is the body's balanced state.

Stressors disrupt homeostasis. The stress response mobilizes psychological and biological processes that work to restore it.

This response is adaptive in the short term because it prepares the organism to meet a challenge. A system that is useful during brief danger can become damaging when activation is prolonged.

---

# 6. Neurobiological Responses to Stressors

Stress responses involve coordinated activity in the **amygdala**, **hippocampus**, and **prefrontal cortex**.

## Amygdala

- The earliest stress responses typically occur in the amygdala.
- The lecture refers to the amygdala as the **"neural watchdog."**
- It rapidly detects and responds to potential danger or threat.

## Hippocampus

- The hippocampus is central to learning and memory.
- It helps place a potential stressor in context by connecting the present situation with memory.

## Prefrontal Cortex

- The prefrontal cortex works to regulate amygdala responses to potential stressors.
- It supports evaluation and control rather than simply producing the initial alarm response.

## Recognition Cues

| Brain Region | Main Lecture Role |
|---|---|
| Amygdala | Early stress response; "neural watchdog" |
| Hippocampus | Learning and memory |
| Prefrontal cortex | Regulation of amygdala responses |

---

# 7. Hormonal and Autonomic Responses

When the brain registers a stressor, it rapidly activates two biological systems.

## Autonomic Nervous System

The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** includes:

- The **sympathetic nervous system (SNS)**
- The **parasympathetic nervous system (PNS)**

The ANS regulates involuntary bodily processes. During a stress response, sympathetic activation helps mobilize the body, while parasympathetic activity is associated with restoring and conserving bodily resources.

## Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis

The **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA axis)** is the second major biological system activated by stress.

The ANS and HPA axis produce the release of **catecholamines** and **cortisol**, helping the body prepare for the stressor.

## High-Yield Sequence

1. The brain registers a stressor.
2. The ANS and HPA axis are activated.
3. Catecholamines and cortisol are released.
4. Energy and bodily resources are mobilized to respond.

---

# 8. Immune-System Responses

Everyday acute stressors can activate the **inflammatory response** of the immune system.

Activation of the inflammatory response involves increased levels of proteins that promote inflammation. From an evolutionary perspective, this response helped the body cope with the increased risk of infection and injury associated with stressors.

## Short-Term Benefit and Long-Term Cost

- In the short term, inflammation can support healing and defence against infection.
- When inflammation becomes chronic, prolonged exposure to inflammatory proteins can contribute to cell death and tissue damage.

The apparent contradiction is resolved by duration: a response that is adaptive during an acute threat may become harmful when it continues for too long.

# 9. Stress and Coping Theory

A major source of individual differences in stress responses is how people **appraise** potential stressors.

## Primary Appraisal

**Primary appraisal** means determining whether a stimulus is a threat.

Central question: **Does this event place important demands on me or threaten something important?**

Examples:

- "This examination is worth 40% of my grade."
- "This event could seriously affect my future."

## Secondary Appraisal

**Secondary appraisal** means determining whether you can handle the stressor.

The person evaluates:

1. Whether the situation can be controlled
2. Whether coping mechanisms and resources are available

If the person has sufficient control and coping resources, the event causes less stress. If the person lacks sufficient resources, the event is more stressful.

Example: "I have enough time, support, and study strategies to prepare for this examination."

## Primary Versus Secondary Appraisal

| Appraisal | Main Question | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Primary appraisal | Is this a threat or meaningful demand? | The demands of the potential stressor |
| Secondary appraisal | Can I handle it? | Control, coping mechanisms, and available resources |

---

# 10. Challenge and Threat Theory

**Challenge and Threat Theory** describes how appraisal determines whether a potential stressor is experienced as a challenge or a threat.

## Lecture Flow

1. A **potential stressor** is encountered.
2. **Primary appraisal** evaluates the demands associated with the perceived stressor.
3. **Secondary appraisal** evaluates the resources available to deal with the potential stressor.
4. The person reaches one of two appraisals:
   - **Threat:** demands exceed resources.
   - **Challenge:** resources exceed demands.

The same event can therefore be a threat for one person and a challenge for another. The difference is not merely the objective event; it is the relation between perceived demands and perceived resources.

---

# 11. Perceived Control: Glass and Singer (1972)

**Glass and Singer (1972)** placed participants in a room and asked them to complete difficult puzzles.

## Conditions

- **No perceived control:** participants experienced unpredictable loud noise and could not turn it off.
- **Perceived control:** participants were told that they could turn the noise off, although they did not actually use the control.

## Result

The no-perceived-control group performed worse.

## Take-Home Message

**Perceived control shapes the stress response.** Actual use of control was not necessary. Knowing that control was available improved later performance.

This finding is closely connected to secondary appraisal because perceived control is one of the resources people evaluate when deciding whether they can handle a stressor.

# 12. Stress and Mental Health

Psychological stressors are widely recognized as major risk factors for common psychological disorders, including **post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)**.

The lecture emphasizes that:

- Stressors involving social rejection are especially likely to trigger depression.
- Loss events are strong risk factors for psychological disorders.
- Humiliation events are strong risk factors.
- Entrapment situations are strong risk factors.
- Danger events are strong risk factors.
- Childhood stressors are particularly harmful and can have long-lasting effects.

A stressor is a risk factor, not a guarantee that a disorder will develop. Predisposition, appraisal, coping resources, and social support also influence the outcome.

# 13. Stress and Physical Health

## Acute Illnesses

Stress may:

- Increase the risk of developing illnesses such as colds.
- Make asthma attacks more severe.
- Reactivate latent viruses, such as herpes simplex virus.

## Chronic Illnesses

Stress may:

- Increase the severity of chronic illness.
- Influence cardiovascular disease through both acute and chronic stress.
- Influence autoimmune disorders, including multiple sclerosis, lupus, and Crohn's disease.

## Critical Interpretation

Stress can affect:

1. The probability that an illness develops
2. The severity of an existing illness
3. The reactivation of a latent condition

These are different claims. Read the wording of an exam scenario carefully.

# 14. The Diathesis-Stress Model

The **Diathesis-Stress Model** proposes that mental and physical disorders develop from a genetic or biological predisposition for an illness, called a **diathesis**, combined with stressful conditions that play a participating or facilitating role.

## Components

- **Diathesis:** an underlying genetic or biological predisposition
- **Stress:** environmental or psychological conditions that help trigger or facilitate the disorder
- **Outcome:** risk is greatest when predisposition and stress occur together

## What the Model Does Not Claim

- Genetics alone do not determine the outcome.
- A stressor alone does not determine the outcome.
- Two people exposed to the same stressor do not necessarily have the same risk.

Example: After the same job loss, a person with a family history of depression and multiple adverse childhood experiences would generally be at greater risk than a person without those vulnerabilities and with strong social support.

## Vulnerability and Plasticity

The lecture contrasts **vulnerability factors** with **plasticity factors**.

- A vulnerability factor increases the harmful effect of adverse conditions.
- A plasticity factor indicates greater sensitivity to environmental conditions, potentially producing worse outcomes in harmful settings but better outcomes in supportive settings.

# 15. Pathways From Stress to Disease

## Chronic Inflammation

Prolonged exposure to inflammatory proteins can lead to:

- Cell death
- Tissue damage

Chronic inflammation is one pathway through which prolonged stress can contribute to mental and physical disease.

## Biological Aging

With **biological aging**, older cells become less able to divide or function. This increases risk for diseases associated with aging, including cardiovascular disease and autoimmune disorders.

## Telomeres

**Telomeres** are DNA-protein complexes that cap chromosomes and protect DNA against damage.

The lecture uses telomeres to illustrate biological aging. Damage or shortening that reduces cellular protection is associated with older, less functional cells and increased disease risk.

---

# 16. General Adaptation Syndrome

The **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)** describes three stages of prolonged physiological response to stress.

## 1. Alarm Phase

The **alarm phase** is the initial, healthy reaction to stress. The body rapidly mobilizes resources to meet the stressor.

## 2. Resistance Phase

During the **resistance phase**, the body adapts to high stress. Non-stress-related processes are shut down so that resources can remain directed toward coping with the stressor.

## 3. Exhaustion Phase

During the **exhaustion phase**, the body can no longer cope with other processes being shut down. Illness, injury, or death can occur.

## Sequence and Recognition Cues

| Phase | Recognition Cue |
|---|---|
| Alarm | Initial mobilization and healthy reaction |
| Resistance | Continued adaptation; other processes are reduced or shut down |
| Exhaustion | Resources are depleted; illness, injury, or death may occur |

Do not confuse the alarm phase with the harmful outcome. Alarm is an adaptive initial reaction; the greatest danger appears when prolonged activation reaches exhaustion.

---

# 17. Reducing Stress and Enhancing Resilience

**Resilience** is the capacity to adapt successfully and maintain or recover functioning in the face of stress.

Protective behaviours may have:

- A **direct effect** on health outcomes
- A **stress-buffering effect**, reducing the negative impact of stress

## Behaviours That Improve Resilience

1. Maintain a physically active lifestyle.
2. Maintain a healthy sleep schedule.
3. Maintain a healthy diet.
4. Maintain social support.
5. Use relaxation techniques.

Social support is not simply pleasant companionship. It can provide emotional, informational, and practical resources, thereby changing secondary appraisal and buffering the effects of stress.

---

# 18. Integrated Stress Pathway

The lecture can be organized into one exam-ready pathway:

1. An internal or external **stressor** is encountered.
2. The brain, especially the **amygdala**, detects potential threat.
3. **Primary appraisal** evaluates demands.
4. **Secondary appraisal** evaluates control and coping resources.
5. The event is experienced as a **challenge** or **threat**.
6. The **ANS**, **HPA axis**, and immune system coordinate the stress response.
7. Short-term activation can be adaptive and help restore **homeostasis**.
8. Prolonged activation may produce chronic inflammation, biological aging, and greater mental and physical disease risk.
9. Resilience behaviours and social support can reduce or buffer these effects.

---

# 19. High-Yield Distinctions

1. **Stressor versus stress:** A stressor is the circumstance or stimulus; stress is the response.
2. **Eustress versus distress:** Eustress is a reaction to good stress; distress is a reaction to bad stress.
3. **Acute versus chronic versus traumatic stressors:** Acute refers to short duration, chronic to endurance over time, and traumatic to an immediate and severe threat to physical integrity.
4. **Yerkes-Dodson Law versus Maximal Adaptability Model:** The first predicts peak performance at moderate stress; the second emphasizes adaptation across underload and overload.
5. **Primary versus secondary appraisal:** Primary appraisal evaluates threat or demands; secondary appraisal evaluates control and coping resources.
6. **Threat versus challenge:** Threat occurs when demands exceed resources; challenge occurs when resources exceed demands.
7. **ANS versus HPA axis:** Both are activated by stress and contribute to the release of catecholamines and cortisol, but they are distinct biological systems.
8. **Acute inflammation versus chronic inflammation:** Acute inflammation can protect against injury and infection; prolonged inflammation can damage cells and tissue.
9. **Diathesis versus stress:** Diathesis is an underlying predisposition; stress is the condition that can participate in or facilitate disorder development.
10. **Alarm versus resistance versus exhaustion:** Alarm is initial mobilization, resistance is prolonged adaptation, and exhaustion is failure after resources and other processes can no longer sustain the response.
11. **Direct effect versus stress-buffering effect:** A direct effect improves health generally; a buffering effect specifically reduces the negative impact of stress.
