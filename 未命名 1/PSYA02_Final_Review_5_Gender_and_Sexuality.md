# 1. Sex

The lecture describes **sex** as biologically determined.

Biological indicators include:

- Genes
- Hormones
- Genitals

Examples presented in the lecture are:

- Male
- Female
- Intersex

## Intersex

**Intersex** refers to biological sex characteristics that do not fit typical definitions of exclusively male or exclusively female bodies.

In an exam question, genes, hormones, genitals, and male/female/intersex categories point toward sex rather than gender.

---

# 2. Gender

The lecture describes **gender** as psychologically and societally determined.

Gender is:

- Related to biology, but not determined by it
- Spectral
- Multifaceted

Gender involves identity and social expectations, not only biological characteristics.

## Gender Roles

**Gender roles** are cultural expectations about how women and men should look and act.

Examples include expectations that men should be aggressive, women should be nurturing, or particular toys and activities should be associated with boys or girls.

## Gender Identity

**Gender identity** is whether a person subjectively feels like a woman or a man.

The key word is **subjectively**: gender identity concerns a person's internal sense of gender.

## Sex Versus Gender

| Sex | Gender |
|---|---|
| Biologically determined in the lecture | Psychologically and societally determined |
| Genes, hormones, and genitals | Identity, roles, and cultural expectations |
| Examples: male, female, intersex | Spectral and multifaceted |

---

# 3. Gender Terminology

## Cisgender

**Cisgender** means having a gender identity that matches biological sex at birth.

Recognition cue: identity and sex at birth match.

## Transgender

**Transgender** means having a gender identity that differs from biological sex at birth.

Recognition cue: identity and sex at birth differ.

## Gender Non-Binary

**Gender non-binary** refers to people who do not identify as either male or female.

## Transgender Woman

The lecture presents a **transgender woman** as a person whose:

- Sex at birth is male
- Gender identity is woman

## Transgender Man

The lecture presents a **transgender man** as a person whose:

- Sex at birth is female
- Gender identity is man

## Gender Dysphoria

**Gender dysphoria** is distress brought about by having a gender identity that does not match one's biological sex.

The lecture notes that this distress is often helped by:

- **Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)**
- **Gender confirmation surgery**

## Critical Distinction

Being transgender and experiencing gender dysphoria are not identical concepts.

- **Transgender** describes a relation between gender identity and sex at birth.
- **Gender dysphoria** describes distress associated with incongruence.

The diagnostic cue for gender dysphoria is distress, not merely a transgender or non-binary identity.

# 4. Interpreting Gender Differences

The lecture describes differences between men and women **on average**.

Statements such as "men are typically higher" or "women are typically higher" refer to group averages. They do not mean that:

- Every man has more of the trait than every woman.
- Every woman behaves in the same way.
- Biology completely determines behaviour.
- The distributions do not overlap.

A correct interpretation is that group means may differ while individual variation and substantial overlap remain.

This distinction is essential when applying the lecture findings. Use words such as **typically**, **on average**, and **more likely**, not **always** or **all**.

# 5. Gender Differences in Aggression

The lecture reports that:

- Men are typically higher in aggression than women.
- Men are seven times more likely than women to commit violent crimes.
- Men are more likely than women to shock other people in experiments.

## Zeichner et al. (2003)

**Zeichner et al. (2003)** found that men were more likely than women to shock others in experiments.

The experimental finding matters because it supplements crime statistics with behaviour measured in a controlled setting.

## Correct Interpretation

The reported difference is probabilistic and average-based. It does not imply that all men are aggressive or that no woman is aggressive.

# 6. Gender Differences in Empathy

The lecture reports that women are typically higher in **empathy** than men.

Women are described as more likely to:

- Show considerate social behaviours, including smiling, expressing interest, and apologizing
- Develop emotionally supportive relationships
- Share more intimate details about their lives with family and friends

**Empathy** concerns understanding and responding to another person's feelings or perspective. In a lecture-based question, considerate behaviour and emotionally supportive relationships are the strongest recognition cues.

# 7. Gender Differences in Sex Drive

The lecture reports that men typically have a higher **sex drive** and are more willing to have casual sex than women.

## Clark and Hatfield (1989)

The lecture presents the following findings from **Clark and Hatfield (1989)**:

| Measure | Men | Women |
|---|---:|---:|
| Attractive stranger offers sex | 75% agreed | 0% agreed |
| Want multiple sexual partners each month | 25% | 3% |
| Pornography use | Six times more likely | Less likely |

## What the Findings Show

These findings show a behavioural gender difference in the social setting studied: men were more likely to accept an offer of casual sex and reported greater interest in multiple partners and pornography use.

## What the Findings Do Not Prove

The findings do not, by themselves, prove a purely biological difference in sex drive. Behavioural responses can also be influenced by:

- Personal safety concerns
- Pregnancy risk
- Social stigma
- Fear of reputational punishment
- Cultural expectations
- Other social consequences

The study is therefore consistent with a difference in willingness under the observed conditions, but explanations for that difference must be evaluated using the evolutionary and sociocultural accounts presented in the lecture.

---

# 8. Three Explanations for Gender Differences

The lecture asks what can account for gender differences in aggression, empathy, and sex drive and presents three explanations.

## 1. Evolutionary Explanation

Natural selection favoured gender differences because particular tendencies increased reproductive or survival success.

## 2. Sociocultural Explanation

Social learning and cultural expectations teach children gender roles. Children copy others and observe which behaviours are rewarded or punished.

## 3. Sex Double Standard

Cultures may praise men but shame women for the same sexual behaviour, creating different behavioural incentives and consequences.

## Comparison

| Explanation | Primary Mechanism | Recognition Cue |
|---|---|---|
| Evolutionary | Natural selection and adaptive benefit | Hunting, fighting, childcare, mate selection, parental investment |
| Sociocultural | Learning through observation, reward, punishment, and social pressure | Copying models, gender roles, toys, approval or disapproval |
| Sex double standard | Different social consequences for the same sexual behaviour | Men praised, women shamed, fear of negative social consequences |

The explanations are not logically mutually exclusive. An exam question should be answered by identifying the mechanism emphasized in the scenario.

---

# 9. Evolutionary Explanation

The evolutionary explanation proposes that natural selection favoured differences when they produced adaptive benefits.

## Aggression

Men benefit from aggression because aggression can help men:

- Hunt
- Fight
- Defend offspring

## Empathy

Women benefit from empathy because empathy can help women become better childcare providers.

## Sex Drive and Mate Selectivity

The lecture proposes that women benefit from lower sex drive because it helps women become more selective in mate choice.

Greater selectivity is presented as especially important because women have higher **parental investment**.

## Parental Investment

**Parental investment** refers to the time, energy, biological cost, and resources devoted to producing and caring for offspring.

Recognition cues for an evolutionary answer include natural selection, reproductive success, survival, childcare, mate selection, and unequal parental investment.

---

# 10. Sociocultural Explanation

The sociocultural explanation emphasizes **Social Learning Theory**.

## Social Learning Theory

**Social Learning Theory** indicates that children learn gender roles by:

- Copying other people
- Seeing which behaviours are rewarded
- Seeing which behaviours are punished

Social learning teaches children to conform to gender roles at an early age.

The lecture describes the following expectations:

- Boys and men are expected to be more aggressive and less nurturing.
- Girls and women are expected to be less aggressive and more nurturing.

## Gender Schemas

**Gender schemas** are organized expectations and beliefs about behaviours, traits, activities, and objects associated with gender.

For example, children may experience social pressure to play with toys that fit gender schemas. Approval reinforces gender-conforming choices; ridicule or punishment discourages choices that violate expectations.

## Scenario Recognition

A child watches an adult, copies a gendered behaviour, receives praise, and repeats it. This is a sociocultural explanation based on Social Learning Theory, not an evolutionary explanation.

---

# 11. Sex Double Standard

The **sex double standard** means that the same sexual behaviour receives different social evaluation depending on whether a man or a woman performs it.

The lecture reports that:

- Men who have multiple sexual partners and engage in casual sex are praised.
- Women who do the same are shamed.

## Cultures Without Gender Equality

In cultures without gender equality, observed differences in sex drive are larger. Compared with men, women are less likely to:

- Engage in masturbation
- Use pornography
- Have casual sex

The lecture connects these differences with fear of negative social consequences.

## Cultures With Gender Equality

In cultures with gender equality, greater similarities in sex drive are observed. Women are more likely to:

- Engage in masturbation
- Use pornography
- Have casual sex

## Interpretation

When gender differences become smaller as gender equality increases, the pattern supports a sociocultural contribution. If a difference were entirely fixed and biologically determined, a large systematic change with social equality would be more difficult to explain.

This is evidence for cultural influence, not proof that biology has no role.

---

# 12. Integrating the Clark and Hatfield Finding

The Clark and Hatfield finding can be interpreted through more than one theory.

## Evolutionary Interpretation

Women may be more selective because of higher parental investment, while men may gain more reproductive benefit from multiple partners.

## Sociocultural Interpretation

Women may reject an unfamiliar person's offer because women face different safety risks, social expectations, stigma, and reputational consequences.

## Sex-Double-Standard Interpretation

Men may be praised for accepting casual sex, while women may be shamed for the same behaviour. Different social consequences can change willingness to accept the offer.

## Exam Conclusion

The result demonstrates a gender difference in behaviour under the study conditions. It does not isolate one cause. A strong short answer identifies the finding and then explains how each theory interprets it through a different mechanism.

# 13. High-Yield Distinctions

1. **Sex versus gender:** Sex is biologically determined in the lecture; gender is psychologically and societally determined.
2. **Gender role versus gender identity:** A gender role is a cultural expectation; gender identity is a person's subjective internal sense of gender.
3. **Cisgender versus transgender:** Cisgender means identity matches sex at birth; transgender means identity differs from sex at birth.
4. **Transgender identity versus gender dysphoria:** Transgender describes identity; gender dysphoria refers to distress associated with incongruence.
5. **Gender difference versus absolute rule:** A typical group difference does not describe every individual and does not eliminate overlap.
6. **Aggression versus empathy findings:** Men were typically higher in aggression; women were typically higher in empathy.
7. **Observed behaviour versus cause:** A behavioural difference does not, by itself, show whether the cause is biological, sociocultural, or both.
8. **Evolutionary versus sociocultural explanation:** Evolutionary explanations emphasize natural selection and adaptive benefit; sociocultural explanations emphasize learning, reward, punishment, and cultural expectations.
9. **Social Learning Theory versus sex double standard:** Social Learning Theory explains broad acquisition of gender roles; the sex double standard specifically emphasizes unequal evaluation of the same sexual behaviour.
10. **Parental investment versus social consequences:** Parental investment is an evolutionary mechanism; stigma and reputational punishment are sociocultural mechanisms.
